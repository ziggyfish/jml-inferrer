\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The single-pass strategy is not subsuming}

Our measurement settles a question the predecessor papers left open: the isolated-pass inferrer's \texttt{InterproceduralAnalyzer}~\cite{edmonds2026inference} does not capture what a compositional refinement would, on any of the five libraries we measured. Two structural shapes are out of reach by
construction: branch-conditional implications that depend on the
caller's control-flow context, and disjunctive obligations from
polymorphic dispatch. The cross-library data shows these are not
edge cases --- branch-conditional implications are the largest single
shape of newly-added preconditions on Lang, IO, and Vavr, and a third
or more on Math and jOOL; disjunctive shapes are present on every
library and most prominent where interface inheritance runs deep
(Vavr, 18.6\%).

The implication for the inferrer's downstream consumers
(test generators, IDEs, LLMs) is that running the second pass before
emission delivers a measurably richer spec. Whether the additional
clauses translate into stronger downstream behaviour (more bugs
caught, fewer false positives in static analysis, better test
inputs) is the empirical question for follow-up work; we report only
that the gap exists and that it is structurally explainable.

\subsection{Why the single pass already does so much}

We also note what the single pass \emph{does} cover. The isolated
baseline is already rich --- a substantial fraction of its clauses
are interprocedural propagations the \texttt{InterproceduralAnalyzer}
produces during isolated inference~\cite{edmonds2026embedding} --- yet
the second pass adds 18{,}854 \emph{new} clauses on top of it: the
single pass does the right thing on unguarded, single-candidate call
sites, and the gap concentrates in the guarded and polymorphic cases.

\subsection{Implications for analyser architecture}

For inferrers that target downstream tools where richer specs help (deductive verification with KeY~\cite{key2016book} or OpenJML~\cite{openjml}, test generation with EvoSuite~\cite{fraser2011evosuite} or Randoop~\cite{pacheco2007randoop}, mutation-testing pipelines~\cite{coles2016pitest,papadakis2019mutation}, or LLM-driven consumers~\cite{schafer2023adaptive,lemieux2023codamosa,kang2023libro}), the second pass is worth integrating: an extra 50 seconds total across all five libraries for an average of 31.47\% of methods receiving refined specifications is a strong trade. For inferrers that target downstream tools that consume
specs eagerly (auto-completion, hover text in an IDE), the
runtime cost may not justify the extra clauses; the second pass
is opt-in by design so deployers can choose.

A single-pass pipeline could still recover the easy half of the
gap: the branch-conditional shape only needs the enclosing guard of
each call site, available during the AST walk the isolated pass
already performs, so the substituted clause could be lifted through
the guard before being added. The polymorphic-dispatch shape is
harder to fold in, as it requires looking up sibling cache entries at
the point of propagation --- exactly what the second pass does.

\subsection{Scope of the result: precondition propagation versus class invariants}
\label{sec:scope-invariants}

The RQ3 finding --- that the second pass's fixpoint iteration never
fires in practice (Section~\ref{sec:empirical}) --- is specific to
\emph{precondition propagation along the call graph} and does not
generalise to every specification a compositional inferrer produces.
Class invariants are the sharpest boundary. The call-graph fixpoint
stays cheap because callee preconditions flow one way, callee to
caller, so reverse-topological order refines each in a single sweep
and the cap (16) is never reached. A class invariant has no such
acyclic structure: it is at once an assumption every method may use
at entry and an obligation every method must re-establish at exit, so
inferring it is a greatest-fixpoint --- weakening a candidate because
one method violates it withdraws an assumption that \emph{other}
methods, even ones with no call edge to the offender, relied upon.
That iteration is driven by shared-invariant coupling, not the call
graph, so ``the fixpoint never fires'' says nothing about it. Our
\texttt{ClassInvariantInferrer} side-steps this by inferring
invariants conservatively in a single local pass --- sound, but
forgoing exactly those whose preservation depends on a callee's
effect. Converging class invariants is thus a distinct contribution
we leave to future work.

\subsection{Limitations}

The 453-line analyser is a scaffold, not a complete WP transformer.
It does not lift through loops (the inferrer's loop-invariant
analyser handles those in the isolated pass), does not reason about
aliasing (the syntactic substitution treats two distinct names as
distinct values), and does not interact with the SMT verifier that
discharges the final clauses. These are the boundaries of the
present contribution; the comprehensive WP transformer would be a
substantially larger development.

The 208-clause worst case (one jOOL method) sits in the deep
generic-combinator hierarchy where many polymorphic and guarded call
sites compound. Whether so large a spec is \emph{useful} downstream
--- parsimonious enough to read, tight enough to inform --- we defer
to the planned downstream-impact study.
