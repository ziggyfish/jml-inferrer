\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\markboth{Abstract}{Abstract}

Formal methods --- the use of precise, machine-checkable specifications to
verify that software behaves as intended --- have been available for decades
and are demonstrably effective, yet their use remains confined to a few niche
domains such as safety-critical and security-critical systems. This thesis
asks why, and whether the situation can be changed. Its premise is that the
binding constraint is not the verification technology, which is mature, but the
manual cost of writing the specifications that technology requires as input:
specifications are expensive to author by hand, the cost scales with the size
of the codebase, and it is paid up front before any benefit is realised. The
thesis investigates whether automatically inferring specifications from
existing code --- removing that cost --- and making applications easier to
verify as a result can broaden the adoption of formal methods beyond their
current niche.

The work is built around \emph{JML-Inferrer}, a static analysis system
developed for this thesis that infers Java Modeling Language (JML) contracts
--- preconditions, postconditions, loop invariants, and frame conditions ---
from Java source by pattern matching organised around the weakest-precondition
and strongest-postcondition calculi, with every inferred clause discharged
\emph{a posteriori} through a formal verifier so that the heuristic front end's
unsoundness is filtered rather than shipped. The first study establishes that
this removes the cost barrier in practice: across 312 methods of a real
library, the engine infers specifications at over 90\% precision against both a
human and a formal oracle, and supplying them to a large-language-model test
generator improves its fault-detection (mutation) score by 40.7 percentage
points over a signature-only baseline, evidence that cheap, automatically
inferred specifications carry real value.

The second and third studies develop a second, more consequential consequence
of inferred specifications for adoption: that they make software's behavioural
\emph{compatibility} analysable at the library boundary. Because a verifier
checks a caller against its callees' contracts, a specification carried with a
compiled library lets a consumer verify client code against a dependency
without that dependency's source; the thesis contributes an embedding mechanism
that carries inferred JML losslessly inside Java bytecode and OpenAPI
descriptions, validated across more than twenty thousand methods at under 11\%
overhead. And because a caller's specification is \emph{derived from} its
callees' specifications, a change to a library propagates into the contracts of
its callers: when a library's inferred specifications change, the compatibility
of client code with the new version becomes a question that can be answered at
the specification level rather than guessed. The thesis measures the mechanism
this rests on --- that caller specifications depend substantially on callee
specifications --- across five libraries and 21{,}052 methods, finding that a
compositional analysis recovers 42{,}370 cross-method obligations a single-pass
analysis cannot express, dominated by exactly the guard-conditional and
dispatch-dependent shapes through which library changes propagate to callers.

Taken together, the studies argue that the adoption barrier formal methods have
long faced is, in significant part, removable: specifications can be inferred
accurately and cheaply, distributed with the artefacts that need them, and made
to propagate across library boundaries so that verification and compatibility
analysis become routine rather than niche.
